# Why should the T-34 tank be studied in every engineering school

The story of the value tank that was simply good enough

## Introduction

In the current context of the war in Ukraine, many people were genuinely surprised when Russia started outproducing the combined capacity of the NATO bloc in terms of 155mm artillery shells.

Surely, Russia was a big country with a sizeable industrial capacity, and surely, they spent a much more sizable fraction of their total GDP on artillery shells, but still, the whole NATO bloc had orders of magnitude higher industrial capacity.

Well, there is one important piece of the puzzle that is missing - the very high accuracy modern artillery shells that are constantly being touted by the media as being superior to anything made before - such as the M982 Excalibur - require GPS navigation. This allows for pinpoint accuracy even close to the maximum range of about 40km. The navigation is embedded in each shell and drives the price per round to between $50K and $100K - depending on the contract, the number bought, the production delays and the shipping schedule. An ordinary 155mm shell - much less accurate beyond 10km - costs about $2K to $4K. Prices always vary a lot, since every large order is negotiated separately with a public bid.

On the other hand, the Russian shells cost $800 to $1K. This is why Russia easily outproduces NATO.

The Russians also have GPS-guided artillery rounds - and these cost only slightly less then their various NATO counterparts. However they use them very sparingly and the bulk of the rounds fired in Ukraine are the older, dumb 155mm rounds. NATO, on the other side, seems to have planned to completely phase out dumb artillery munitions.

It is probably time to remind one of the most important engineering lessons of WW2 - one that came out of the USSR and came to a huge surprise to Germany - in their overconfidence in their own technical superiority.

The T-34 is the war's most produced tank and the second most produced tank in history (the most produced one was its Cold War-era successor, the T-55). It is widely considered to be an incredibly successful design that was instrumental in turning the war in USSR's favor.

So what made this tank so good?

## The design ideology

The Soviet tank doctrine called for a simple, cheap and reliable tank that was going to be built in very large numbers and to be handled by an inexperienced crew with little training. The Soviets considered that only three elements were important in a tank design: the penetrative power of the main gun, the thickness of the frontal armor and its cross-country mobility. Everything else was secondary or even a waste of resources.

*It was precisely this ideology - the cost vs value tank - that became the key to the incredible Soviet tank success on the battlefield. The Germans, convinced in their superiority, always pursued the perfect design. The Soviets had correctly understood the basic principle of industrial warfare - tank warfare was essentially a competition of industrial output.*

## The origins of the T-34 tank

The development of the T-34 tank starts 10 years before WW2. Tank warfare emerged during the last stages of WW1 and together with aviation went through an era of great development during inter-war period - driven by the exponential adoption of the combustion engine. The Germans - who during WW1 lagged behind the Entente in terms of technology - were the first to recognize the importance that tanks and aircraft were going to play during the next war. To their greatest surprise, it was the Soviets who ranked a close second in this race.

Paradoxically, it was the Germans who helped them bootstrap their tank industry. Severely restricted by the Versailles Treaty, the Germans decided to turn to the USSR for their tank development, building a shared research center on Soviet territory. A key component of this collaboration was a naturally-aspired V-12 BMW diesel engine, originally built as an aircraft engine, from which the Soviets inspired to built their tank engine which they called the Kharkiv model V-2.

A second key component came from the US where an engineer, called J. Walter Christie, was trying to build tanks for the US government. The US government never bought them and he died ruined, but the Soviets recognized the ingenuity of his suspension - the famous Christie suspension - as well as his sloped glacis armor which dramatically increased the effective armor without adding extra weight. They licensed the tank and incorporated his design ideas into the BT-7 - the T-34's predecessor.

By this time the Soviets had completely closed their technological gap and their BT-7 tank was an excellent modern tank on par with the French and British tanks. They were also able to gain some early experience in tank warfare during the Khalkin Gol campaign - something both the French and British lacked. This is what allowed them to further improve their design.

## BT-7 grows into T-34

BT-7's armor was deemed far too light and fragile during the undeclared war with Japan in Khalkhin Gol, so the Red Army decided to upgrade the armor. Initially the T-34 was considered far too heavy, complicated and expensive - thus contrary to the Soviet doctrine. Still, Mikhail Koshkin, its chief designer, was able to convince Stalin that this was a good and reliable tank by driving two preproduction units 750km from their factory in Kharkiv (modern Ukraine) to Moscow to show off during a party meeting in January 1940. Stalin agreed and gave the order to mass produce these. After the fall of France a few months later, it was decided to produce a staggering total of 12 000 units.

None of the original designers of the T-34 lived to see its glory. Its original chief designer, Mikhail Koshkin, died in 1940 from pneumonia on the way back during the gruelling winter drive to Moscow. Konstantin Chelpan, the designer of the V-2 engine was purged and executed in 1938. Innokenty Khalepsky, the head of the Red Army tank department who met Christie and discovered his tank design was also purged and executed in 1938. His successor, Adolf Dick (an ethnic German) was arrested and sent to the Gulag camps after he failed to meet his production quotas. The only one to survive the war was the designer of the transmission - the very famous weak spot of the early units - who after the death of Koshkin became the new chief engineer and created the later models - Aleksandr Morozov. According to classical Soviet thinking, those purged were removed from Soviet history books.

## The German tanks - Panzer III and Panzer IV

The Germans were very proud of their automobile industry, which they considered to be the best in the world. They had invented both the petrol and the diesel engine. However, because of the restrictions of the Versailles treaty, at the start of the war, their Panzer I and Panzer II tanks were vastly inferior to the French and British tanks. In fact, even the Czech tanks, which they got for free when the Allies did nothing to stop the invasion of Czechoslovakia were far better than their own. They used these extensively during the invasion of France where they won through surprise, cunning, risk taking and innovative maneuver warfare.

It was not until the invasion of the USSR and the new generation of tanks - the Panzer III and the Panzer IV - that the Germans were finally able to build good tanks. However this was the first time that they were going to experience actual real and prolonged tank-to-tank warfare.

## Key differences

Direct comparisons of tanks are always very difficult because as the war progressed, tanks on both sides were constantly upgraded. Still, there was a number of key differences that were very important and must be mentioned. First of all, the German tanks used mostly petrol engines which tended to be more agile, but were also more prone to catching fire, while the Soviet tanks used mostly diesel engines. This German decision also turned to be a very bad one, since Germany had a permanent oil-shortage problem because of the British naval blockade.

Still, the German tanks had a number of significant advantages:
* The German tanks were much more ergonomical, later models had a very advanced steering differential and used steering wheels, while the T-34 used two clutches controlled with two sticks which made steering the tank very difficult. It required great skill and sometimes strength to maneuver. On early T-34 models, equipped with a 4-speed gearbox, passing the 3rd and the 4rd speed required applying up to 50kg of force - sometimes requiring the help of another crew member.
* Each German tank was equipped with a radio, while only command T-34 tanks had radios, individual combat tanks had to rely on signalling with flags during combat.
* The German tanks had very good optics and precision mechanics giving them excellent accuracy and range. During combat, the German tanks usually fired first, from a greater distance and with greater accuracy.
* The German tanks usually had large numbers of highly technological features absent from other tanks of the era such as gun stabilizers, smoke dischargers, grenade launchers with a periscope or anti-magnetic mine coatings.

On the other side, the Russian tanks:
* The Russian tanks had very good sloped glacis armor which often deflected hits from the smaller German guns, while shots from the powerful Russian main guns were absolutely devastating for the German tanks.
* The very wide tracks of the T-34 and its Christie suspension gave them excellent cross-country performance while the German tanks struggled in mud - the famous Russian *rasputitza*.
* The T-34 was a true multirole battle tank with two types of rounds (explosive and armor-piercing) while during the early stages of the war, the Germans used WW1-style tactics with a dedicated infantry-support tank (Panzer IV) and an anti-tank tank (Panzer III), during the later stages they switched to a multirole design starting with the Panzer IV F2.

**Once again, the key Soviet doctrine is obvious: a cheap tank built for mass production that is good enough. It does only three things, but does those three things very well: fires powerful shots, resists well enemy hits and is able to drive cross-country well. Nothing more, nothing less.**

## Quality and reliability

Tank reliability during WW2 is always a complicated and often a hotly debated topic. There is a common misconception that the German tanks were far too complex and unreliable - which is not entirely true. During the opening stages of the war, the German tanks were of excellent quality. The German tanks were able to easily drive over huge distances without suffering mechanical breakdowns. The early T-34, which were rushed in production, often by inexperienced workers brought en masse from the farms to huge newly built factories, were of abysmal quality. The T-34 was planned to have an MTBF of 200 hours or 300km. At the end of 1941, during tests at the factory, it was determined that on average, only 7% of the brand new tanks were able to drive 300km without suffering from some kind of mechanical breakdown that prevented them from continuing without qualified repairs.

However, improving the MTBF during the early war was not considered a top priority by the Red Army. It was already higher than the average survival time of the tank in combat. Producing more tanks was what mattered the most.

The German tanks tended to be maintenance hogs and required lots of work to keep them running. There was no such thing as maintenance on the T-34. It was essentially a disposable tank. It could eventually be cannibalized for parts when it broke down.

A typical example were the tracks. The tracks of a tank are made of steel segments held together by steel pins. The pins on the German tracks had a locking mechanism, while the pins of the T-34 were simply hammered into place and tended to fall out after driving long enough. However the Red Army tank bureau came to the conclusion that the average time needed for the pins to fall out was vastly inferior to its average survival time in combat and did not change the design until much later during the war - when they simply added a small bump on the hull. When the tracks passed through this bump, it tended to slightly push the pins that were falling out. This extended the average lifespan of the tracks beyond the desired MTBF and the design remained.

In 1942, as part of the Lend-Lease act, the Soviets sent 2 T-34s to the US. The US Army Ordnance Department was eager to see the tank that was turning the tide on the German armor which had steamrolled Western Europe. They were in disbelief when they finally saw it. The quality of the welding was so bad, the US Army Ordnance Department noted that during heavy rain, water tended to infiltrate and knock out the electrical circuits. This was not considered a problem worthy solving by the Red Army - the number of tanks lost due to heavy rain was far too small to consider improving the welding. It was precisely because of the large numbers of inexperienced welders that were brought to the factories that they were able to produce such great numbers of T-34s.

The Germans, on the other hand, sanded their welds.

Still, the Americans quickly understood the concept, and while the M4 Sherman was an independent design, it too sacrificed performance for reliability and ease of maintenance and incorporated many ideas from the T-34. It solved its worst problem - the fact that the stored ammunition exploded in a fiery inferno far too easily when the tank was hit from the back - the famous wet ammo storage of the Sherman - but because of their lack of actual tank combat experience, they introduced a host of new problems.

The Red Army never addressed the exploding ammo problem - few tanks got hit from the back. It was cheaper to replace them.

As the war progressed, the Soviets gradually gained more experience and their workers eventually learned to weld properly. During the same quality inspections in 1944, it was found out that the number of T-34 leaving the factory that were able to drive 300km without breaking down had surpassed 50%. At the same time, due to wartime shortages of materials and rushed production, the quality of the German tanks had fallen dramatically. In 1944, it was the Soviets who drove their tanks over large distances and the Germans who struggled to keep theirs running.

The Germans were very slow to recognize their strategic mistake when it came to tanks. They encountered the T-34 for the first time on the day following the invasion. At first, they were surprised by its good armor. Then it came the main gun. And finally, to their greatest dismay, there were the numbers.

Their only relief was its construction quality. The first runs of T-34 were so bad, that most of the tanks they destroyed or captured had spare transmission axles attached behind their turrets - since the breakdown ratio was over 50% after a few hours of pushing heavily the engine in combat.

Then, gradually, the construction quality started to improve.

*Improving construction quality is always a very difficult, slow and tedious process which require utmost dedication, patience and discipline. But it is definitely possible. Sacrificing build quality for time-to-market, and then trying to improve, is certainly a valid strategy.*

## Kill ratios

Kills ratios are equally very difficult to give. The Germans boasted that they had a 3:1 kill ratio and this is what their military planning used. In reality, during the early stages of the war, the kill ratio was nearly 7:1, but most of these tanks were older obsolete BT-7s. During the last stages of the war, the kill ratio was sometimes as low as 1.5:1. Once again, kill ratios are very difficult because these tended to vary greatly from battle to battle and very often most of the destroyed tanks were destroyed from the air - tanks are very vulnerable to air attacks and this is something that hasn't changed up to this day.

The fact that the Germans held the upper hand in tank kill ratio until the very last moment is generally attributed mostly to the fact that their tanks were easier to drive, their crews tended to be better trained and more experienced, and they usually had better combat cohesion.

Soviet crews had low survivability and were very frequently rookies. This, combined with a very hard to drive tank, was their worst problem.

## Production

After the Germans invaded Western Europe, the Soviets planned to build 12 000 T-34s to be completed by May 1942. Various production setbacks and mismanagement delayed the production and by the time the war started they had built only about 2000 (to check?).

The Germans invaded with about 3 500 tanks - which they considered to be a staggering amount - more than any other nation had previously built. In the very famous Mannerheim recording, Hitler admits that they never expected this amount of tanks on the Soviet side. By December 1941, the Germans had destroyed 9 000 tanks - most of them obsolete BT-7s and T-26s. This is the moment when they started encountering mostly the T-34 - which was far more difficult to destroy.

In fact, at the eve of the invasion the USSR had about 20 000 tanks.

The first one to run the numbers on the German side was Fritz Todt. However Hitler had zero tolerance policy for dissent when it came to his unrealistic war in the East and there are suspicions that his plane crash might not have been an accident.

The Germans rushed to upgrade their tanks - their main guns, their armor and their production capacity. They factored in their unrealistic 3:1 kill ratio and decided that for the 600 T-34s that the Russians were building per month, they needed 200 Panzers. They greatly expanded the main production facility - the Nibelungenwerk in Austria - where a large portion of the tanks were coming from. The production rate reached 300 units per month, with 100 of these alloted to the North African campaign.

However, in reality, the Russians were building on average 1200 T-34s per month in 1942 - reaching 1500 in some months. And the 3:1 kill ratio was absolutely unrealistic.

By the end of the war, the Soviets would built a total of 85 000 tanks between the original T-34 and the upgraded T-34/85. More than half of these would be destroyed in combat.

The Germans will build only 8500 Panzer IVs and this will be Germany's most produced tank.

**The truth is that the Russians had simply the better tank design.**

The T-34 was initially built in two major factories - one in Kharkiv (modern Ukraine), where it was designed, and another one in Stalingrad (modern Volgograd). The one in Kharkiv was transferred to the East - during the great moving of their essential industry East (a very fascinating, but separate subject), since Kharkiv was overrun by the Germans very early in the war. The Germans managed to destroy the one in Stalingrad during the battle for the city - it was a heroic event during which tanks went directly into combat from the production line. As the war progressed, there were many more factories that were built in major cities.

Serial production was key, but the Soviets were not fanatical about it. The German tanks had small differences depending on the manufacturing factory. On the Russian side, everyone was building whatever they could. There was the turret cast in one piece - because that's what they were good at in Stalingrad. There was also the hexagonal turret welded from different pieces, because they knew how to weld in Kharkiv. These were two quite different tanks, but they still carried the same designation. At one point the Moscow factory did not have V-2 engines available and started producing petrol T-34s - using tractor engines they happened to have on hand. These were also called T-34.

## Upgrades

The Panzer III had 12 main variants - designated by letters from A to N and 5 more specialized derivatives. The Panzer IV had 10 main variants and about 15 specialized derivatives. The T-34 had 4 models: 1940, 1941, 1942 and 1943. It was a multirole vehicle that was also used for construction, towing, clearing mines and troop transport. The Germans had a specialized vehicle for each of these tasks.

When the Germans finally started recognizing the superiority of the T-34, they designed a completely new tank, repeating their initial mistake. The Panzer V Panther, considered a revolutionary tank, the precursor of the modern main battle tank, was superior to the T-34 in everything. Its frontal armor resisted direct hits from the T-34. Its main gun easily destroyed the T-34. It had interleaved track wheels which gave it excellent cross-country performance and featured a very advanced hydraulic gun stabilization which allowed firing on the move with very little loss of accuracy.

The Panther costed about 55 000 man-hours to build. The T-34 had started at about 8000 in 1941, but experience reduced this to less than 4000 in 1943.

The legendary Panther VI tank, the famous Tiger, costed close to 300 000 man-hours. It had very little impact on the war.

When they encountered the Panther for the first time, the Soviets decided that the most cost-effective counter was to upgrade the main gun - creating the T-34/85. The new tank was slightly more expensive but now the Panther was not invulnerable and if each Panther encountered 3 T-34/85s, it was sure to lose. And this is precisely what happened on the battlefield. They had very correctly deduced that this tank was going to be far too expensive to be made in great numbers.

When the Germans finally rushed to drive the cost of their tanks down, the build quality suffered enormously and late German tanks had horrible MTBF.

*Improving the production quality of a cheap product is difficult, but possible. Transforming an expensive design into a cheap one is not.*

## Conclusion

When Germany invaded the USSR in 1941, both countries had a somewhat similar industrial output - with Germany having far less population and much higher per capita output. The Germans supposedly had the higher hand in automobile technology - but in reality their design lacked severely in value. Ultimately, it was the Honda Civic, and not the Rolls Royce, that won the war. The total war boiled down to a simple competition in industrial output, and this competition was mostly about cost-efficiency - and not about having the most advanced design in the world.
